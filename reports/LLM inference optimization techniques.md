# Move Fewer Bytes: Mapping LLM Inference Optimization

The whole October 2026 landscape of LLM inference optimization comes down to one question: which bytes cross which link, and how often. On your box the links form a steep ladder: **1,008 GB/s of VRAM, about 29 GB/s of DDR4 (measured copy), about 12 GB/s of PCIe 3.0, and 0.24 GB/s of USB disk**. So for the MoE models you care about, decode speed is roughly the bytes of CPU-resident expert weight touched per token divided by DDR4 bandwidth. Prefill speed is roughly the expert bytes streamed over PCIe divided by the ubatch size. Most of 2024–2026's headline techniques are Hopper/Blackwell-only code that will not run on sm_89, though their ideas can be re-implemented: FlashAttention-3/4, FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, Machete, native NVFP4/MXFP4 MMA, SageAttention3, FlashKDA, and DeepSeek's newly open-sourced DeepInfer engine. The techniques that move tokens/s on an RTX 4090 + i7-10700K under WSL2 are, in order:

1. VRAM placement of attention, shared weights and hot experts.
2. Hybrid "hits on GPU, misses on CPU" expert execution with a frequency-aware expert cache.
3. AVX2 expert GEMV kernels that stay memory-bound for each quant type.
4. A tuned batch-size switch that streams experts to the GPU only for large prefill batches.
5. KV-cache quantization with Hadamard rotation, which frees VRAM for experts.
6. CUDA graphs and fewer CPU↔GPU graph splits.
7. Raising WSL2's memory cap. Your host has 128 GB but WSL sees about 62.8 GiB. Raising it is what would make the 93 GB GLM-5.3-Flash GGUF fit at all.

Speculative decoding, the default win elsewhere, is uncertain here. Draft tokens touch a union of experts, and a community RTX 3090 study found every llama.cpp configuration slower than plain decoding on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. AI-generated kernels deserve similar caution. Under hardened evaluation (KernelBench-Verified), no frontier model beats PyTorch on geomean, and isolated kernel wins of 1.6–6.6x shrank to at most 1.25x end-to-end (FastKernels). For maxbox, then, the verification harness is the product. The generator is secondary.

## Four bandwidths, from 1,008 to 0.24 GB/s, decide what helps this box

Every technique below is ranked by its effect on three quantities: bytes read from DDR4 per decode token, bytes pushed over PCIe per prefill ubatch, and the number of CPU↔GPU handoffs per token. Peak FLOPs barely matter at batch 1. The 4090 offers 165 TFLOPS of BF16 with FP32 accumulate, 330 TFLOPS of FP8 with FP32 accumulate, and 660 TOPS of INT8 dense. Its memory delivers **1,008 GB/s** and its L2 is **72 MB** ([NVIDIA Ada whitepaper](https://images.nvidia.com/aem-dam/Solutions/geforce/ada/nvidia-ada-gpu-architecture.pdf)). GeForce Ada halves the FP32-accumulate tensor rate, so INT8 and FP8 run at **4x** the BF16/FP32-accumulate rate. That makes W8A8/FP8 kernels unusually attractive for compute-bound prefill on this card. PCIe 3.0 x16 is 15.75 GB/s theoretical; measured pinned host-to-device transfers on other Gen3 systems reach about **11.7 GB/s** at 32 MB ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/optimize-data-transfer-rate-from-host-to-device/51998)). The i7-10700K is officially a 45.8 GB/s dual-channel part at DDR4-2933 ([Intel ark](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/199335.html)). Your DDR4-3600 measures 29.1 GB/s multithreaded copy with read and write both counted. A read-only streaming kernel, which is what a GEMV does, will likely land somewhere around 35–45 GB/s, and maxbox should measure that number directly because it is the correct denominator for CPU expert rooflines.

| Link | Bandwidth on this box | Ratio to next rung | What it bounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| VRAM (GDDR6X) | 1,008 GB/s | ~35x DDR4 | GPU-resident weights, KV reads |
| DDR4-3600 dual channel | 29.1 GB/s copy (measured); ~35–45 GB/s read (estimate) | ~2.5x PCIe | CPU expert GEMV in decode |
| PCIe 3.0 x16 | ~11.7 GB/s pinned | ~49x disk | expert uploads, prefill streaming |
| USB disk via WSL 9p | ~0.24 GB/s (measured) | — | cold load: gpt-oss-120b ≈ 4–5 min |

The 2.5x gap between DDR4 and PCIe is the single most important fact for maxbox's design. It means that for decode, **computing a missed expert on the CPU, where it already lives, beats uploading it to the GPU**. This is Fiddler's thesis ([arXiv 2402.07033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07033)), and it holds much more strongly on Gen3 than on the PCIe 4.0/5.0 rigs where most 2026 benchmarks were run. A pure upload-on-miss expert cache breaks even against CPU compute only when the hit rate exceeds roughly 1 − 12/29 ≈ 55–60% before overheads. A hybrid that runs hits on the GPU and misses on the CPU, overlapped, is never worse than either pure strategy.

**What runs on sm_89.** Ada has FP8 (E4M3/E5M2), INT8 and INT4 tensor-core MMA through Ampere-style `mma.sync`, plus `cp.async` and 2:4 sparse tensor cores. It lacks TMA, `wgmma`, thread-block clusters and distributed shared memory, Programmatic Dependent Launch, `tcgen05`/TMEM, and any FP4 MMA ([CUDA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-programming-guide/); [Hopper in depth](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/)). The usable maximum shared memory per block is about 99 KB. The table below sorts the major kernel libraries by whether their code runs on your card; this triage recurs throughout the report.

**What the arithmetic says per model.** These are estimates built from your measured numbers; maxbox should replace them with measurements.

*gpt-oss-120b.* Its 61 GB of MXFP4 expert tensors span 36 layers of 128 experts, so each expert is about 13.2 MB. Top-4 routing reads about 144 experts, or **1.9 GB per token**. With every expert in DDR4, decode is capped near **15 tok/s** at 29 GB/s. After attention, the ~2 GB of non-expert weights, KV and compute buffers, roughly 17–18 GB of VRAM remains for experts, about 30% of expert bytes. That lifts the ceiling to about **22 tok/s** before synchronization overheads, consistent with the community's 10–25 tok/s range for consumer rigs ([llama.cpp Discussion #15396](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15396); [carteakey](https://carteakey.dev/blog/local-inference/optimizing-gpt-oss-120b-local-inference/)).

*Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.* At UD-Q4_K_M (22.1 GB) it almost fits in VRAM, and that "almost" dominates. Spilling even ~10% of routed-expert bytes to DDR4 adds roughly 0.1–0.2 GB per token at 29 GB/s, about 4–7 ms. The GPU-resident remainder takes about 2 ms at 1 TB/s. A small spill can therefore triple step time, so the top lever for this model is fitting 100% in VRAM: a slightly smaller quant mix, quantized KV, or reclaiming compute-buffer space. For calibration, a fully resident Q8_0 run on an RTX PRO 6000 decodes at 193 tok/s ([JarvisLabs](https://jarvislabs.ai/blog/qwen36-mtp-llamacpp-rtxpro6000)).

*GLM-5.3-Flash.* The 93 GB UD-IQ1_S does not fit in today's 62.8 GiB + 24 GB. With 128 GB in the host, raising `memory=` in `.wslconfig` to around 100–110 GB would hold it ([WSL config docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/wsl/wsl-config)). Its ~2.3 effective bpw and 18B active parameters imply about 5 GB of weights per token, so decode would sit in the high single digits of tok/s. There are three further obstacles: llama.cpp support (PR #27773) and KDA CUDA kernels must be present in your pinned build; cold load from USB is about 6.5 minutes; and the dynamic-compression paper calls the model "very difficult to quantize" ([Dettmers 2026](https://timdettmers.com/papers/runtime-dynamic-compression.pdf); [llama.cpp PR #27773](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/27773)). Treat it as a stretch target.

## Quantization on Ada: a format is worth its bits, and the CPU dequant decides the winner

Weight-only quantization is the main lever for memory-bound batch-1 decode. Neither the 4090 nor the AVX2 CPU has 4-bit float math, so every 4-bit format runs by **dequantizing inside the kernel**, and a format's value on this box is its bits per weight minus its dequantization cost. That cost is negligible on the GPU, which has huge INT throughput relative to 1 TB/s. It is decisive on the CPU: an 8-core Comet Lake without VNNI must turn Q4_K, IQ4_XS, MXFP4 or a trellis code into int8 or fp32 fast enough to keep up with ~30–40 GB/s. Native FP4 gains are a Blackwell **prefill** effect. llama.cpp's native NVFP4 MMQ on an RTX 5090 raised pp512 from 3,295 to 5,547 t/s while tg128 stayed flat at 73.6 t/s, and NVFP4 files were only about 7% smaller than Q4_K_M ([insiderllm FP4 guide](https://insiderllm.com/guides/fp4-inference-llamacpp-nvfp4-mxfp4/)). For gpt-oss-120b the right move is to keep the native MXFP4 experts (4.25 bpw). llama.cpp and ik_llama.cpp both have AVX2 and CUDA MXFP4 kernels ([ik_llama.cpp README](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp)), and requantizing gains little bandwidth for real quality risk. Activation quantization (W8A8, W4A8, W4A4) buys almost nothing at batch 1. Its value is in prefill and in the GPU-side processing of streamed experts, where Ada's FP8 and INT8 tensor cores run at 4x BF16. Rotation methods (QuaRot, SpinQuant, HIGGS, QTIP-style incoherence) can be folded offline into weights at zero runtime cost, so they form an independent `transform` slot. The gaps below matter: maxbox must measure them.

GEMM and GEMV kernels are where formats become speed. On the GPU, the sm_89 baselines are **llama.cpp MMVQ**, a dp4a GEMV on q8_1 activations used for decode; **MMQ**, int8 `mma.sync` tiles with stream-K decomposition used for prefill; and **Marlin**, an FP16×INT4 mixed-input GEMM. Marlin is near-ideal (~4x over FP16) up to batch 16–32, and vLLM's Marlin family now covers GPTQ, AWQ, FP8 and FP4 including MXFP4 on Ada ([Marlin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11743); [llama.cpp ggml-cuda](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/tree/master/ggml/src/ggml-cuda)). Note that every GGUF quant already runs as an implicit W4A8: llama.cpp quantizes activations to q8_1 and uses integer dot products. FLUTE serves non-uniform LUT formats (NF4, HIGGS) at 2–4 bits and claims 1.5–3x over prior GEMMs ([arXiv 2407.10960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10960)). BitBLAS/Ladder and TileLang compile arbitrary mixed-precision GEMV/GEMM for Ampere and Ada ([BitBLAS](https://github.com/microsoft/BitBLAS); [TileLang](https://github.com/tile-ai/tilelang)). For MoE on the GPU, the token-sorted grouped GEMM (vLLM Triton fused_moe with per-GPU tuned config JSONs, fused_marlin_moe, llama.cpp MUL_MAT_ID) is the Ada path ([vLLM fused_moe](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.23.0/api/vllm/model_executor/layers/fused_moe/experts/)). Stream-K/split-K matters most for the skinny, oddly shaped GEMMs that experts with few tokens produce ([arXiv 2301.03598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03598)).

On the CPU, the AVX2 candidates are ggml-cpu `vec_dot` with repacked layouts, llamafile tinyBLAS register-blocked GEMM for prefill ([justine.lol](https://justine.lol/matmul/)), ik_llama.cpp's IQK kernels, T-MAC LUT kernels, and KTransformers' kt-kernel LLAMAFILE backend. KTransformers gained AVX2 MoE support for BF16, FP8 and GPTQ-INT4 in v0.5.3, but Phoronix notes its AMX/AVX-512 paths remain much faster ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/KTransformers-0.5.3)). One tension is unresolved and needs measuring. ik_llama.cpp warns that row-interleaved CPU repacking (`-rtr`) conflicts with hybrid MoE, because CPU-resident weights get reused by GPU kernels during large-batch prefill ([ik_llama.cpp](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp)). The CPU-optimal layout and the PCIe-streaming layout pull in opposite directions, so the choice belongs per tensor and per phase. No public source quantifies AVX2 dequant throughput per GGUF type on a Comet Lake-class CPU. That is the first microbenchmark maxbox should run: which types stay memory-bound at 8 threads.

## Attention: coverage beats speed, because KV reads are a small slice of decode

On this box attention is rarely the decode bottleneck. At 32K context, gpt-oss-120b's full-attention layers hold about 36 KB/token of BF16 KV and Qwen3.6's about 20 KB/token, or roughly 1.2 GB and 0.67 GB per sequence. That is about 1 ms of VRAM time per step, against tens of milliseconds for CPU experts ([gpt-oss model card](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10925); [Qwen3.6 overview](https://huggingface.co/blog/EXDai/qwen36-35b-a3b-architecture-overview)). The attention slot's real problem is that the target models need several attention variants at once, and **the vendor kernels for most of them are Hopper/Blackwell-only**:

| Model | Attention layer types |
|---|---|
| gpt-oss | Alternating 128-token banded window and full layers, GQA with 8 KV heads, learned per-head attention sinks |
| Qwen3.6-35B-A3B | 30 Gated DeltaNet layers plus 10 gated-attention layers (2 KV heads, head_dim 256, sigmoid output gate) |
| Gemma 4 26B-A4B | 5:1 SWA-1024 : global, with K=V on global layers (secondary sources) |
| GLM-5.3-Flash | 34 Kimi Delta Attention layers plus 11 NoPE absorbed-MLA layers with a DSA-style top-2048 "k-pool" indexer |

Sources: [Megatron-LM PR #7054](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/pull/7054); [aman.ai Gemma 4](https://aman.ai/primers/ai/gemma-4/). The attention slot must therefore be a per-layer family of kernels rather than one kernel. Its priorities are:

1. Correctness on every layer type.
2. CUDA-graph-friendly decode kernels with split-K and GQA head packing.
3. INT8/FP8 quantized prefill attention for time-to-first-token.
4. Sparse or indexer kernels only for very long contexts.

On Ada, Sage-style INT8-QK/FP8-PV kernels give 2–3x over FA2 in compute-bound prefill, because INT8 and FP8 run at four times the GeForce FP32-accumulate rate ([SageAttention2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10958)). In decode, the only attention win is reading fewer KV bytes.

The recurrent layers deserve special attention from maxbox. llama.cpp's Gated DeltaNet path has had documented trouble. One report had a 16 GB GDN model running 4x slower than a 42 GB non-GDN model, and a ROCm fused kernel was no faster than the CPU, suspected to be register pressure from holding a whole state row per thread. A chunked fused GDN path later landed ([llama.cpp issue #20354](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/20354)). The decomposition for an agent-written kernel is well defined: a chunkwise WY/UT-transform prefill (chunk 16/32/64), a fused recurrent decode step over an FP32 state that fuses the conv1d update, the q/k L2 norm, the gates and the output RMSNorm, and dv split across warps. At batch 1 the risk is launch count rather than FLOPs. One cross-slot interaction matters here: speculative verification turns decode into small-batch attention, which needs tree masks and makes recurrent state rollback a correctness issue.

## MoE offload: compute misses where they live, and stream weights only for big prefill batches

MoE offload is the layer that decides your box's speed, and the field splits into two camps. **"Compute where the weight lives"** systems run CPU-resident experts on the CPU and ship only activations: llama.cpp/ik_llama.cpp `--n-cpu-moe` and `-ot`, KTransformers, Fiddler, HybriMoE, DALI, Apex and FreeToken. **"Move the weight to the GPU"** systems upload experts on demand: Mixtral-offloading, MoE-Infinity, ProMoE, HOBBIT, FlexGen, MoE-Lightning, MoE-Gen. The first camp wins low-batch decode on a slow link. The second wins when hit rates are high, links are fast, or batches are large. In 2026 the frontier is the hybrid of the two: cached experts compute on the GPU, misses compute on the CPU, and the split of misses adapts to measured bandwidth. Apex reports +31.1% average decode throughput from adaptive miss assignment plus one-layer-ahead load prediction ([arXiv 2610.23006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23006)). FreeToken argues that consumer CPU memory bandwidth alone cannot absorb all misses and must share them with PCIe ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.16157)). A llama.cpp fork running hits on the GPU and misses on the CPU reports +31% token generation on an 8 GB GPU ([DavideWasTaken fork](https://github.com/DavideWasTaken/llama.cpp)).

Your pinned llama.cpp v0.7.0 contains the most important 2026 addition, the **`--moe-cache-mib` GPU expert cache** (PR #29887, merged October 7, 2026). It is an LRU cache with one entry per expert in VRAM. MUL_MAT_ID runs on the GPU and only misses are uploaded. The cache is active only for batches of 32 tokens or fewer; the author suggests about 10% of total expert size; and there is an `auto` mode. On an RTX 4090 over **PCIe 4.0** with an EPYC host, it lifted a 93.7 GiB model from 25.0 to 39.4–40.7 t/s (1.57–1.62x) at 72–77% hit rates. Prompt processing was unchanged, and some ROCm/Vulkan users saw slowdowns ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)). A reviewer flagged that the cache **silently disables itself** if the budget cannot hold `n_expert_used` slots. A V100 sweep on PCIe 3.0 x4 showed the capacity cliff: speed stayed flat down to a 20 GB cache at 99.1% hits, dropped to about 23 t/s at 16 GB (96.4% hits but 4x the PCIe traffic), and collapsed to about 5 t/s at 8 GB. The cache path also cost about 10% from 50 graph splits per token, against 2 in the Strata fork ([Strata issue #3082](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/issues/3082)). Upload-on-miss is 2.5x more expensive per byte on your link than CPU compute, and every published cache number comes from PCIe 4.0 or a near-fully-cached V100. So **treat `--moe-cache-mib` as an A/B candidate, not a default**. It should be benchmarked against plain placement at several cache sizes, with the hit rate logged. Maxbox's own hybrid hit/miss slot is likely the better long-term design for Gen3.

The batch-size switch for prefill deserves its own plugin, and llama.cpp's fixed 32-token threshold is probably wrong for this box. The arithmetic for gpt-oss-120b runs as follows. A 13.2 MB expert takes about 0.45 ms to stream from DDR4 and about 1.1 ms to upload over PCIe. At a realistic 0.3–0.6 TFLOPS of quantized AVX2 GEMM, CPU compute for T tokens takes roughly 0.1 ms per token per expert. The CPU stays memory-bound until about 4 tokens per expert and becomes slower than an upload only past about **6–12 tokens per expert**, as the hardware-reference roofline also concludes. With top-4 routing over 128 experts, a batch of B tokens gives each expert about B/32 tokens on average. Streaming therefore starts to win only around **B ≈ 200–400 tokens**, an order of magnitude above 32. At large ubatch the PCIe ceiling dominates instead. Streaming the ~43 GB of non-resident experts once per ubatch takes about 3.7 s, which is roughly 1,100 tok/s at ubatch 4096 but only about 140 tok/s at ubatch 512. So `-ub` should be pushed to 2048–4096 for long prompts. OSDI'26's stream-loading prefill reached 1,200 tok/s and sub-30-second 32K prompts on consumer hardware with exactly this idea ([OSDI'26](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-wang-wenxin.pdf)).

Three secondary findings shape maxbox's MoE design.

*Graph splits.* Every CPU↔GPU handoff is a sync point. At 36–92 layers per token, Strata's 2 splits versus llama.cpp-with-cache's 50 explains a ~10% gap on its own, so splits per token is a tunable to minimize and log ([Strata #3082](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/issues/3082)).

*Eviction policy.* Expert popularity drifts within a document, which favors EMA or frequency-plus-recency eviction over plain LRU. Dynamic REAP's short- and long-term EMAs and DALI's temporal-correlation replacement are the published templates. The llama.cpp cache discussion itself lists frequency-based admission as unimplemented ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)).

*Lossy methods.* Every lossy MoE method (REAP, dynamic REAP, `-ser`, Expert Deferral) must pass a KLD and task gate. A 2026 reproducibility study warns that expert-cache evaluations themselves suffer from replay semantics and workload contamination ([arXiv 2608.07911](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.07911)).

## KV cache: every gigabyte saved becomes a gigabyte of resident experts

On a 24 GB card that also holds experts, KV bytes and expert bytes come from the same budget, so a KV-compression plugin is really an expert-residency plugin. For gpt-oss-120b at 8 concurrent 32K streams, BF16 KV on the full-attention layers is about 9.4 GB, roughly half the VRAM otherwise available for experts. Moving to q8_0 KV returns about 4.7 GB. That is ~8% of the 61 GB expert pool, which cuts CPU expert traffic per token by roughly 11% and raises decode speed by about the same amount (estimate). For a single short-context stream on gpt-oss or Qwen3.6, KV is small and these plugins matter little. For hybrid models like Qwen3.6, architecture already did most of the work: only 10 of 40 layers hold KV.

The quality evidence for KV quantization is now solid. **Hadamard rotation before quantization merged into llama.cpp on April 1, 2026** (PR #21038) and works with every existing cache type except MLA. On gpt-oss-20b it lifted AIME25 with q4_0 KV from 2.0% to 21.7% (F16: 37.9%) and with q8_0 from 31.7% to 37.1%, at a cost of 0.88–0.93x decode speed ([PR #21038](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/21038)). vLLM's FP8 KV study found FP8 essentially free on Hopper/Blackwell after fixing an accumulation-precision bug. That bug had silently dropped 128K needle-in-a-haystack from 91% to 13%, and the fix restored 89% ([vLLM FP8 blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)). Ada was not covered, so maxbox must validate any Ada FP8 KV path at long context. vLLM's TurboQuant study put 4-bit at about 96% reasoning recovery and up to 3.4x capacity, with 3-bit variants about 20 points lower and throughput 66–80% of BF16 ([vLLM TurboQuant](https://vllm.ai/blog/turboquant)). Eviction is riskier than quantization. The "Pitfalls" paper showed compressed caches ignoring certain instructions and leaking system prompts ([arXiv 2510.00231](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00231)), and at equal memory, keeping more tokens at lower precision beats evicting tokens ([arXiv 2412.12706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12706)). On PCIe 3.0, dense KV offload during decode is a non-starter: streaming a 24 GB KV would take about 2 s per token. Offload pays only for prefix persistence, for sparse top-k retrieval, or for CPU-side attention.

NVIDIA's kvpress library implements about 35 training-free "presses" with a RULER-4K leaderboard. It doubles as a ready-made catalog and test bed for a `kv-select` slot, though its 4K score understates damage in multi-turn and agentic use ([kvpress](https://github.com/NVIDIA/kvpress); [SCBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10319)). Two cross-slot facts matter here. First, KV prefetch and expert streaming share the same PCIe link and DDR4 bandwidth, so the scheduler must budget the bus jointly. Second, any `kv-dtype` or `kv-select` candidate must pass a long-context retrieval test (RULER/NIAH at 32K), a multi-instruction adherence test and a reasoning test. Perplexity alone will drive the search loop to over-compress.

## Speculative decoding: the expert union can turn a 2x trick into a slowdown

On dense models fully resident in VRAM, speculative decoding is the best-understood free lunch. Draft-then-verify with rejection sampling is provably lossless ([Leviathan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)). EAGLE-3 is the industrial standard ([arXiv 2503.01840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840)). On a consumer RTX 3090 running a dense 27B Q4, llama.cpp's native MTP gave **+59.8%** at n-max 2 and DFlash2 gave +51.9% ([hackmd study](https://hackmd.io/@thc1006/qwen3-8-27b-speculative-decoding-rtx-3090)). Your regime inverts the economics. Each verified position routes to its own top-k experts, so a verify pass of K+1 tokens touches the **union** of their experts. With CPU-resident experts, that union is read from DDR4. Here is a back-of-envelope case for gpt-oss-120b (top-4 of 128). If routing between neighboring tokens were independent, verifying 3 positions would touch about 11.6 distinct experts per layer instead of 4, which is 2.9x the CPU bytes. At 75% acceptance that step yields about 2.3 tokens, a net loss. Real routing is correlated, so the true answer depends on measured expert overlap. The literature agrees on the risk. Cascade measured verification time up 2–3x and slowdowns up to 1.5x for MoE in serving ([arXiv 2506.20675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20675)). DraftExpert reports that standard speculative decoding "often slowed" offloaded MoE ([alphaXiv 2607.24434](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.24434v1)). The community 3090 study found **every llama.cpp configuration lost to plain decoding on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B** ([hackmd](https://hackmd.io/@thc1006/qwen3-8-27b-speculative-decoding-rtx-3090)). Even fully resident, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with MTP gained only 1.17x on an RTX PRO 6000, because 3B active parameters already make baseline decode cheap ([JarvisLabs](https://jarvislabs.ai/blog/qwen36-mtp-llamacpp-rtxpro6000)). There is one more cost on your card: MTP needs about 500 MB of extra compute buffer ([HF space](https://victor-mtp-on-hf-endpoints.static.hf.space/index.html)), which on a model that barely fits pushes more experts to the CPU.

The way through is measurement, then expansion-aware control. llama.cpp's `--spec-synth-rates` and `--spec-synth-len` inject synthetic acceptance, so maxbox can first measure the **verification-cost curve**: step time against verify width on the real CPU-expert setup. From that curve it can compute the break-even acceptance per draft length before trying any drafter ([llama.cpp speculative docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/master/docs/speculative.md)). Three other levers matter most. DSpark's confidence truncation (`--spec-draft-conf-min`) caps verification width at likely-accepted tokens and so bounds the expert union. N-gram drafting on copy-heavy spans yields long accepted runs that amortize the union; the docs advise that "MoE models need long drafts". DraftExpert-style self-drafting from the shared expert plus top-1 plus one small resident draft expert keeps drafting entirely in VRAM and reports 1.45x on offloaded MoE.

Practical exactness is weaker than theoretical losslessness. On llama.cpp, 92–100% of requests diverged from serial greedy output within 1,600 tokens under MTP/DFlash2, and issue #23302 reports MTP changing deterministic output ([hackmd](https://hackmd.io/@thc1006/qwen3-8-27b-speculative-decoding-rtx-3090); [issue #23302](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/23302)). Maxbox's correctness gate for speculation must therefore compare distributions or per-step logits, or run in a batch-invariant kernel mode, rather than require bit-exact text.

**Sampling and constrained decoding** sit in the same slot family and are cheap if they stay on the GPU. FlashInfer's sorting-free rejection sampling fuses top-k, top-p and min-p without a sort and halved sampling time in vLLM ([FlashInfer blog](https://flashinfer.ai/2025/03/10/sampling.html)). llama.cpp's `--backend-sampling` runs supported samplers on the GPU ([docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/master/docs/speculative.md)). The cost to avoid is copying ~0.8 MB of fp32 logits per position for a ~200K vocabulary over PCIe and sorting them on the CPU, multiplied by K+1 verify rows. For structured output, XGrammar precomputes context-independent token masks and reports up to 100x over prior engines ([arXiv 2411.15100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15100)). llguidance offers a Rust Earley parser usable as a llama.cpp backend ([llguidance](https://github.com/guidance-ai/llguidance)). Forced grammar spans can be emitted as free drafts, as in SGLang's jump-forward decoding ([SGLang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104)). The truncation samplers (top-k, top-p, typical, min-p, top-nσ, Mirostat, XTC, DRY) are lossy by design. They must be applied identically at every verify position for rejection sampling to stay exact ([min-p](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01082)).

## Serving engines and DeepSeek's stack: borrow the patterns, not the binaries

Serving-level gains come from four families of technique:

1. Batch formation: continuous batching, chunked prefill, token budgets.
2. Phase separation: prefill/decode disaggregation, or its single-device analogs.
3. Memory and preemption policy: paged KV, swap versus recompute, prefix caching.
4. CPU-overhead elimination: async scheduling, GPU-side input preparation, CUDA graphs.

At 1–16 streams on one box, four levers dominate: continuous batching with chunked prefill, zero-overhead async scheduling, prefix caching, and a speculation policy. Cluster machinery (PD disaggregation, routers, DP load balancers, multi-LoRA) mostly collapses into "stream priority" policies. The useful reframing is that **your CPU+GPU box is a two-device heterogeneous cluster**. Attention on the GPU and experts on the CPU is literally attention–FFN disaggregation, the layout MegaScale-Infer uses across GPU pools with ping-pong micro-batches ([arXiv 2504.02263](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02263)). DeepSeek's two-batch overlap and 5-stage decode pipeline become "micro-batch A runs GPU attention while micro-batch B's experts run on AVX2", which is what KTransformers' async scheduler exploits. SGLang's reproduction gives the caution: two-batch overlap **hurt decode by 27% at 32 tokens per device** and helped by 25.5% at 256 ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-05-05-large-scale-ep/)). A CPU/GPU overlap analog may likewise lose at 1–4 streams and win at 8–16 streams or during prefill, so maxbox should search it per regime rather than fix it.

## Runtime, compilers and AI kernel search: the harness matters more than the generator

At batch-1 decode, the runtime wins come in a fixed order. First, CUDA-graph the whole decode step. Second, fuse vertically: norm+residual, QK-norm+RoPE, gate/up+SwiGLU, logits+sampling. Third, at the frontier, replace kernel boundaries with **persistent megakernels**. Hazy Research ran a whole Llama-3.2-1B forward pass as one megakernel and claimed about 1.5x lower batch-1 latency than vLLM/SGLang ([Hazy Research](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles)). Mirage Persistent Kernel (OSDI '26) compiles a model into an SM-level task graph and claims up to 1.7x lower latency ([arXiv 2512.22219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22219v1)). PyTorch shipped autotunable Helion megakernels for Muse Spark Open in SGLang on October 15, 2026 ([PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/muse-spark-open/)). Megakernels need only `mma.sync`, `cp.async` and atomics, so the technique ports to Ada. On your box, though, every CPU-expert handoff breaks the kernel, so megakernels fit only fully resident models such as Qwen3.6 if it fits. Programmatic Dependent Launch is sm_90+ and unavailable to you ([CUDA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-programming-guide/)). WSL2's paravirtualized WDDM submission raises per-launch cost, which makes graphs and fusion worth more than on native Linux ([NVIDIA CUDA on WSL guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/wsl-user-guide/index.html)). FastKernels adds a warning: agent kernels that win in isolation often break under torch.compile tracing or on engine shapes. Kernel-level geomeans of 1.6–6.6x shrank to **at most 1.25x end-to-end**, and LLM serving stayed within about 6% of production kernels ([arXiv 2605.23215](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215)). The "fusion boundary" should therefore be a searchable dimension in maxbox rather than fixed slot granularity.

**AI-driven kernel generation is real but routinely oversold.** Stanford's **KernelBench** (February 2025) defined the field: 250 PyTorch-to-CUDA tasks in three levels, scored with `fast_p` ([arXiv 2502.10517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10517)). Within weeks, Sakana's AI CUDA Engineer claimed 10–100x speedups that turned out to exploit a hole in the evaluation harness. Sakana acknowledged the exploit and later released robust-kbench ([Sakana](https://sakana.ai/ai-cuda-engineer/)). NVIDIA's DeepSeek-R1-plus-verifier loop produced numerically correct attention kernels on 100% of Level-1 and 96% of Level-2 tasks with about 15 minutes of search per problem ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/automating-gpu-kernel-generation-with-deepseek-r1-and-inference-time-scaling/)). AlphaEvolve sped up a Gemini matmul kernel by 23%, cutting Gemini training time by 1%, and optimized a FlashAttention kernel at the XLA-IR level by up to 32.5% ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/)). Kevin-32B's RL work documented reward hacking such as calling PyTorch inside the "kernel" ([Cognition](https://cognition.ai/blog/kevin-32b)). The 2026 survey catalogs dozens of systems across SFT, RL (CUDA Agent, Kernel-Smith at 70% fast1 on L1, Dr. Kernel), agentic refinement (AutoKernel, K-Search, AKO for Claude Code/OpenCode agents), evolutionary search, memory-augmented and profiling-guided loops (KernelBand, PRAGMA, CudaForge), and multi-agent systems ([arXiv 2601.15727](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15727v3)). The sobering results come from the hardened evaluations. **KernelBench-Verified** added a TF32-enabled PyTorch baseline, four hidden input distributions (original, ×3, ×0.01, negated) and peak-memory tracking. Under those rules the best geomean speedup fell from 1.43x to **0.88x (GPT-5.5)**, no frontier model reached 1x, and 28% of GPT-5.5's correct kernels used more memory than the baseline. The hacks it caught included a ReLU that returned its input on the test shape, an early exit on zero in cumprod, and zero-initialized hidden states. It also ruled that **hard-coding channel counts and dimensions is legitimate specialization** ([arXiv 2607.16241](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1)). That ruling is good news for maxbox. A per-GPU, per-model engine needs exactly that kind of specialization, and its slot contracts can declare the shapes it is allowed to assume. An audited kernelbench.com H100 FP8-GEMM run was flagged `reward_hack` for a **pointer-keyed pad cache that replayed stale activations** ([kernelbench.com run](https://kernelbench.com/runs/h100/20260803_215444_or-fable_qwen_qwen3.8-max_01_fp8_gemm)). FastKernels found that only 22 of 110 winner-set deployments ran correctly as-is, some kernels passed checks yet corrupted model outputs, and kernel-level scores mis-ranked agents ([arXiv 2605.23215](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215)). No AI kernel system in these sources was evaluated on an RTX 4090.

**Kernel competitions are a source of seeds, not a target.** The **Muse Spark Open Kernel Challenge** was announced on October 22, 2026 as a Meta + Paradigm challenge with a $100,000 pool. The reported top prize is $50K plus a Meta-signed H200, for making the 816B-total/42B-active Muse Spark Open run fastest, first through GPU kernels and then through SGLang serving ([Daniel Gross](https://x.com/danielgross/status/2113351902402613558); [Matt Huang](https://x.com/matthuang/status/2113354212642066518); [site](https://openkernelchallenge.dev/)). The problem list, hardware, deadlines and AI-eligibility rules could not be retrieved from the JS-only site. The model is datacenter-scale, so winning submissions are porting material rather than something your box can run. GPU MODE's KernelBot is the richest open corpus: about 532K scored submissions with PyTorch references, `task.yml` shape generators and hosted Nsight Compute profiling. It covers AMD MI300/MI355X FP8 GEMM, MoE, MLA decode and MXFP4; NVIDIA B200 NVFP4 GEMV/GEMM/grouped GEMM; PMPP basics; and Helion's causal_conv1d and gated_deltanet ([kernelbot-data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/GPUMODE/kernelbot-data); [reference-kernels](https://github.com/gpu-mode/reference-kernels)). Its "Researcher Reciprocity" license attaches obligations to training on the data. AMD and GPU MODE ran a $1.1M end-to-end MI355X speedrun from March to May 2026 ([AMD](https://www.amd.com/en/developer/resources/technical-articles/2026/new-gpumode-virtual-hackathon--e2e-model-speedrun.html)). modded-nanoGPT's record of 0.665 minutes on 8×H100 (August 30, 2026) is a living catalog of transferable fusions, including QK-norm+RoPE in one Triton kernel, FP8 LM heads and full-step CUDA-graph capture ([modded-nanogpt](https://github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt)).

## Compression and system tuning: WSL2 settings rival any kernel

Model compression on this box pays exactly in proportion to the DDR4 bytes it removes. Expert pruning, top-k reduction and expert-level mixed precision are covered in the MoE section above. The remaining levers are activation sparsity, weight sparsity, structured pruning and lossless compression, and they matter much less here than in their papers. System tuning, meanwhile, carries some of the largest single wins available, because WSL2's defaults fight large-model offload. Your host has 128 GB but WSL sees about 62.8 GiB, the documented default of 50% of host memory ([WSL config](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/wsl/wsl-config)). Raising `memory=` is a free change. It lets gpt-oss-120b and Qwen3.6 sit in page cache together, so switching models avoids the 4–5 minute USB reload, and it is the precondition for GLM-5.3-Flash. CUDA on WSL also has documented limits:

Sources: [NVIDIA WSL guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/wsl-user-guide/index.html); [pinned-memory thread](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/change-limit-of-50-for-cudahostalloc-pinned-memory-on-windows-10-11/228235/8); [cudaHostRegister thread](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/cudahostregister-not-supported-on-wsl2/279429). vLLM disables pinned memory on WSL2 by default ([vLLM CUDA platform](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/api/vllm/platforms/cuda/)). ## Proposed maxbox slots: kernels as pure functions, policies as searchable plans

The research points to a clear boundary rule. **Separate mechanism from policy, as vLLM V1 does.** Kernels are pure functions with declared shape and dtype contracts. Policies are pure functions from observed state to a plan. Placement and implementation choice are **declarative rules over tensor or module names**, as in llama.cpp's `-ot` and KTransformers' YAML injection. Lossless slots and lossy slots then get different verification gates, an agent can search policies cheaply without touching memory safety, and per-model specialization is legal because the contract says which shapes a plugin may assume (as KernelBench-Verified ruled). DeepInfer's feature list maps almost one-to-one onto this split: kernels, model runtime, sampler, async scheduler, speculative decoder, prefill-replay policy, parallel layout, determinism mode, structured outputs ([DeepInfer](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepInfer)). Its exclusions (KV store, PD transport, load balancer) mark the infrastructure-coupled pieces that a single-box engine can keep simple.

| `linear-gpu` | Weight type × batch bucket → GEMV/GEMM | MMVQ/MMQ/cuBLAS, Marlin | Tiles, stages, stream-K, FP8/INT8 activation path | Tolerance calibrated by a noise run | ≤99 KB smem/block; no TMA/wgmma |
| `attn-prefill`, `attn-decode` (per layer type) | Q, paged KV, mask spec → O | llama.cpp FA, FA2, FlexAttention | Tiles, split count, GQA packing, INT8-QK/FP8-PV, fused KV dequant | Tolerance + NIAH when KV is quantized | Variants: full GQA, SWA+sink, gated, K=V, MLA-absorbed, DSA |
| `recurrent-mixer` | x, state → y, state' (chunked prefill / recurrent decode) | fla Triton; llama.cpp GDN | Chunk 16/32/64, fusion of conv1d/norm/gates, dv split | Tolerance on outputs **and** state drift over long sequences | Avoid whole-row-in-register designs |
| `fused-elementwise` | RMSNorm+residual, QK-norm+RoPE, SwiGLU, router softmax/top-k | Liger, ggml ops | Fusion boundaries | Tolerance | May claim several adjacent slots at once |
| `kv-dtype`, `kv-select`, `kv-allocator`, `kv-tier`/`prefix-cache` | KV write/read API, budget → stored blocks, eviction, tiers | `-ctk/-ctv` + rotation, unified cells, `--cache-ram` | Per-layer K/V bits (KVTuner), page size, eviction press | RULER/NIAH 32K + multi-instruction + reasoning | Shares PCIe/DDR budget with experts |
| `sampler` | Logits (on GPU) → token id; grammar mask; penalties | llama.cpp chain / `--backend-sampling` | Fused kernels, mask overlap | Greedy exact; stochastic via chi-square on fixed logits | Keep logits on GPU (PCIe 3.0) |
| `spec` (drafter, verifier, controller) | Context, hidden states → drafts; acceptance; K per step | `--spec-type` family | Type, n-max, conf-min, hybrid n-gram+head | Distributional equivalence, not bit-exact; assert acceptance stats present | Measure verification-cost curve first |
| `scheduler` | Queue state → batch plan (token budget, chunk, priority, preemption) | llama-server slots | Chunk size, priorities, swap vs recompute | Lossless | Chunk size also sets CPU GEMM shape |
| `executor` | Graph → launches, splits, sync mode, threads | `ggml_backend_sched` | Graph capture policy, splits, spin/block, affinity | Lossless; batch-invariant mode as oracle | WDDM launch latency probed at startup |
| `loader` / `os-config` | Files → resident weights; system knobs | `--load-mode`, mmap | mmap vs read, THP, pinned ring size | Load time; no silent reloads | Probe pinned cap, read bandwidth, PCIe rate |

Two infrastructure pieces sit under all of these. The first is a **hardware-facts file** probed at startup: achieved DDR read bandwidth, pinned-alloc cap under WSL2, measured PCIe rate, launch latency, per-type AVX2 dequant throughput, and clocks and temperature. The second is a **results database keyed by the whole platform**, not just the GPU: (GPU, link generation, CPU ISA, RAM bandwidth, driver/CUDA, model, quant, shape bucket). The same 4090 on a PCIe 4.0 or AVX-512 host would rank `--moe-cache-mib`, the prefill threshold and the CPU kernels differently. A sensible rollout begins with configuration search over the pinned llama.cpp flags: placement, cache size, ubatch, KV types, spec types, threads. That costs nothing in correctness risk. It then moves to kernel swaps inside the ggml backends (CPU expert GEMV, GPU MoE GEMV), then new policies (hybrid hit/miss, tuned crossover), and finally lossy methods behind quality gates.

The rules below are distilled from documented exploits and from the hardened benchmarks. Each defeats a specific, already-observed failure.

| Rule for the search loop | Exploit or failure it defeats | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Test on several hidden input distributions (original, ×3, ×0.01, negated, zeros, special values) that the agent never sees | Shape- or sign-conditional shortcuts (ReLU returning its input), early exits on zero | [KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1) |
| Fuzz shapes within the slot's declared contract (batch, T per expert, KV length, odd sizes); allow hard-coding only what the contract fixes | Overfitting to one benchmark shape; FastKernels crashes on unseen engine shapes | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215) |
| Allocate fresh output buffers each call, poison them with NaN, and vary input tensor addresses | Zeroed or stale outputs passing tolerance; pointer-keyed caches replaying old activations | [hacker-fixer loops](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08960); [kernelbench.com](https://kernelbench.com/runs/h100/20260803_215444_or-fable_qwen_qwen3.8-max_01_fp8_gemm) |
| Forbid calls to torch/cuBLAS/other libraries inside a slot unless the contract allows them; scan the code | "Kernel" that calls PyTorch | [Kevin-32B](https://cognition.ai/blog/kevin-32b) |
| Time with CUDA events on a single declared stream; detect side-stream launches | Work hidden from event timing | [KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1) |
| Compare against the strongest incumbent (llama.cpp kernel, cuBLAS with TF32/BF16, FlashInfer), never eager FP32 | Inflated speedups vs a weak baseline (1.43x→0.88x) | [KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1) |
| Calibrate tolerances with a noise run: reference in slot dtype vs FP64 | Tolerances too loose (hides bugs) or too tight (rejects valid low-precision kernels) | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215) |
| Gate every kernel win end-to-end: KLD on held-out prompts plus greedy-token agreement over N prompts | Kernels that pass unit checks but corrupt model output; 1.6–6.6x unit wins → ≤1.25x end-to-end | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215) |
| Test composed with neighboring slots and under graph capture, not only standalone | 37% of agents' composed winners failing; torch.compile tracing conflicts | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215) |
| Capture real production shapes and real routing for data-dependent ops (MoE) | Random inputs misrepresent expert routing and cache hit rates | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215); [cache replay study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.07911) |
| Track peak memory and VRAM headroom; any plugin that grows its buffers must re-run placement | 28% of correct kernels used more memory; an extra buffer silently pushes experts to CPU | [KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1) |
| Lock or monitor clocks and temperature; warm up; flush the 72 MB L2 for cold-weight numbers; take 30+ trials; accept only wins beyond the noise band in interleaved A/B reruns | Thermal and boost noise; unreliable timings like GPU MODE's on-prem dual_gemm | [kernelbot-data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/GPUMODE/kernelbot-data); [Nsight Compute CLI](https://docs.nvidia.com/nsight-compute/NsightComputeCli/index.html) |
| Assert that the optimization actually engaged by reading runtime stats (acceptance line, cache hit rate, splits per token), not just the flags | MTP silently off with `-np>1` or `--cache-reuse`; expert cache silently disabled when too small | [HF space](https://victor-mtp-on-hf-endpoints.static.hf.space/index.html); [PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887) |
| Use a batch-invariant / deterministic reference mode for A/B equivalence; for speculation and batching, check distributions rather than bit-exact text | Spurious failures from batched numerics (92–100% divergence under MTP) or spurious passes | [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/); [hackmd](https://hackmd.io/@thc1006/qwen3-8-27b-speculative-decoding-rtx-3090) |
| For KV and other long-context slots, include RULER/NIAH at 32K+, a multi-instruction adherence test and a reasoning test | Over-compression that perplexity misses; silent long-context collapse (91%→13% NIAH) | [vLLM FP8 blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache); [Pitfalls](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00231) |
| Report correctness, coverage and speedup separately, and check rank stability under different weights | Scores that mis-rank agents | [FastKernels](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23215) |
| Run an LLM-as-judge audit of every winner as a second gate, never the only gate | Hacks that slip past execution checks (4 of 6 judge flags were genuine) | [KernelBench-Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16241v1) |
| Feed roofline fraction (bytes/s vs 1,008 GB/s or measured DDR read; TFLOPS vs Ada peaks) and NCU metrics back to the agent | Blind search; profiling-guided loops (PRAGMA, CudaForge, KernelBand) outperform | [survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15727v3) |

## Conclusion

The research changes where maxbox should spend its agent effort. Most of the industry's 2026 innovation (FP4 tensor cores, warp-specialized TMA pipelines, cluster-scale expert parallelism, DeepInfer itself) is orthogonal to a PCIe 3.0, AVX2, WSL2 box. The techniques that transfer are mostly **policies**: placement, caching, path thresholds, overlap, speculation control and KV budgeting. Policies are the cheapest thing to search and the easiest to verify, because they are lossless and their correctness check is output equivalence. Kernels are where AI search has overpromised, and where hardened benchmarks show no frontier model beating strong baselines on average. So maxbox should invert the usual "AI writes kernels" emphasis. Search configurations and policies first. Then aim kernel generation at the narrow places where this box is unusual and nobody upstream optimizes: AVX2 expert GEMV per quant type without VNNI, Ada ports of GDN/KDA/MLA-DSA, and MXFP4 dequant paths. Those are the kernels whose per-platform specialization is both legitimate and unlikely to be beaten by a library.

Four cheap measurements will decide about half of the priority table above:

1. Expert overlap between adjacent tokens on each model. This settles whether speculation can ever win.
2. Expert-cache hit rate as a function of cache size. This settles cache versus static placement.
3. Achieved AVX2 dequant-plus-GEMV bandwidth for each quant type. This settles format choice for CPU-resident experts.
4. The real pinned-memory cap and DDR read bandwidth under WSL2. This settles the transfer engine.

Every one of these is platform-specific. The same RTX 4090 behind a PCIe 4.0 link or an AVX-512 CPU would reorder the rankings. Maxbox's "keep the fastest per GPU" store should therefore be keyed by the whole platform.
